How to Choose the Right Laterite Stone for Your Project

A practical guide to picking the right size, grade, and supplier — and avoiding the seven most common mistakes Kerala homeowners make.

📅 Updated Jan 2026 · By Red Earth Co. team · 10 min read

Laterite stone is one of the oldest, most versatile building materials in South India. The temples of Belur and Halebidu are built from it. So is the boundary wall of your neighbour's new villa. But the difference between a wall that lasts 50 years and a wall that crumbles in 10 comes down to choosing the right stone from the start.

This guide is built from a decade of supplying laterite to projects across Kerala and Bangalore — and from the most common mistakes we've seen homeowners and contractors make. If you read it end to end, you'll know more about laterite than 90% of the people buying it.

1. Start with your wall, not the stone

The stone you need depends entirely on the wall you're building. Before talking to a supplier, answer these three questions:

  • Is the wall load-bearing? (i.e., does it support a roof slab, floor, or another wall above it?)
  • Will it be exposed or plastered?
  • How tall and how long? (Dimensions drive the quantity and the size)

Load-bearing walls need dense, structurally-graded stone — typically 8×8×14. Compound walls and garden walls can use lighter 7×7×13. Foundation and plinth courses need 15×6×4.

If your engineer or architect hasn't specified a size, ask them before ordering. "The contractor will decide" is how walls end up with the wrong stone.

2. Understand the three standard sizes

There are three sizes of laterite that cover 95% of Kerala construction:

7" × 7" × 13" — Compound & partition walls

Light, easy to handle, faster laying. Used for compound walls, garden walls, internal partitions, and any wall that doesn't carry a load. ~11 pieces per square foot. Most affordable.

8" × 8" × 14" — Load-bearing walls (most common)

The workhorse. Used for villa external walls, internal load-bearing walls, and any structural wall up to G+1. ~9 pieces per square foot. This is what most Kerala homes use for the bulk of their construction.

15" × 6" × 4" — Foundation & heavy walls

Maximum density and compressive strength. Used for foundation courses, plinth bands, retaining walls, and any application where the stone carries significant weight or is in constant contact with moisture.

3. Inspect the stone before you buy

Whether you visit the quarry, the yard, or accept delivery on site, here's what to look for:

Visual checks

  • Colour: Deep red-brown = high iron content = denser, more weather-resistant. Paler yellow-brown = lower iron = softer.
  • Texture: Tight, fine grain is better than coarse, open grain. Open grain absorbs more water and weathers faster.
  • Cracks: Avoid stones with visible cracks, especially running through the middle.
  • Uniformity: Every stone in the batch should be roughly the same size. Mixed sizes mean the supplier isn't sorting.

Physical checks

  • Tap test: Strike two stones together. A ringing sound = dense, good. A dull thud = softer, lower grade.
  • Weight test: A standard 8×8×14 should weigh 8–9 kg. If it feels noticeably lighter, it's lower density.
  • Water test (advanced): Drop a small amount of water on the stone. Dense stone will bead; porous stone will absorb quickly.

4. The 7 most common mistakes

Mistake 1: Buying on price alone

Cheaper laterite is cheaper for a reason — lower density, more breakage, faster weathering. The cost difference between the cheapest and a good grade is ₹8–12 per piece. Over a full villa, that's ₹40,000–₹80,000 — a fraction of the build cost, and the difference between a wall that lasts 50 years and one that needs work in 15.

Mistake 2: Using the wrong size for load-bearing walls

7×7×13 looks similar to 8×8×14 but is significantly weaker. Putting it under a load-bearing wall is a structural risk. Always match the size to the structural requirement.

Mistake 3: Skipping the plinth band

The plinth is the most exposed part of any wall — it sees rising damp, splash-back, and the brunt of weathering. Using 15×6×4 for the plinth band (the lowest 1–2 courses) protects the wall above. Skipping this is the #1 reason laterite walls fail early.

Mistake 4: Buying from brokers, not quarries

Brokers aggregate stone from multiple quarries, often mixing grades and sizes. They add 10–20% markup. Working with a direct quarry owner (like us) gives you consistent quality, transparent pricing, and supply reliability.

Mistake 5: Ignoring delivery logistics

Stone that's "₹3 cheaper per piece" but takes 5 days longer to deliver is a false saving if your masons are sitting idle. Factor in delivery time and reliability, not just rate.

Mistake 6: Under-ordering

Always add 5–7% extra for wastage. Stone breaks in transit, during on-site handling, and at cutting. Under-ordering means a second small load — which costs more per piece and delays the project.

Mistake 7: Storing stone badly on site

Stack on level ground, off the bare earth (use wooden planks or pallets), and cover if rain is expected. Poor storage wets the stone unevenly, causing shrinkage cracks when it dries in the wall.

5. Red flags when choosing a supplier

  • They can't (or won't) let you visit the quarry
  • They quote a price without asking about size, grade, or quantity
  • They can't tell you which quarry the stone is coming from
  • They demand 100% advance with no terms
  • They don't issue GST invoices
  • The price is significantly below market (if it sounds too good, the stone is too soft)

6. Green flags of a good supplier

  • They own the quarry (or work exclusively with one)
  • They grade and sort at the source, not at delivery
  • They'll send samples or arrange a site visit
  • They offer phased delivery for large projects
  • They have a track record — references, project gallery, repeat clients
  • They provide proper documentation (GST invoice, transport manifest, weight ticket)

7. The bottom line

Good laterite is a 50+ year material. Bad laterite is a 10–15 year material. The difference comes down to three things: choosing the right size, inspecting the stone, and working with a supplier who cares about consistency.

Do those three things right and your walls will outlast you.
